# INSPIRE: Conducting Good Community Partner Interviews

In INSPIRE, interviews help your team understand the community context in which your software will be used and develop a shared understanding of what the project should achieve. Community partners bring knowledge of their field, their organization's work, and the communities with whom they work. Their experience can help you recognize needs, question assumptions, interpret information, and assess whether a proposed solution would be useful in practice.

**Treat community partners as collaborators whose knowledge should shape the project throughout development.** An interview is an opportunity to learn how they understand the situation, including things your team would not know to ask about. You bring software development knowledge; your partner brings expertise that helps determine what is worth building and how it should work.

The interview process has five parts:

- **A: Preparing** for the interview
- **B: Planning and scheduling** the interview
- **C: Opening and closing** the interview
- **D: Conducting** the interview
- **E: Following up** and applying what you learned

## A. Preparing for the Interview

### Learn about your partner and the project

Before the interview, review the project brief, your partner's website, and any relevant materials they have already shared. Look for information about their mission, current activities, community relationships, existing processes, and intended project outcomes. Familiarize yourself with the terminology they use.

Use this preparation to ask informed questions and make good use of your partner's time. Avoid asking them to repeat information that is already available unless you need clarification. Public materials provide a starting point; the interview can help you understand how things work in practice and what those materials leave out.

### Identify what you need to learn from their expertise

Discuss these questions as a team:

- What do we already know, and where did that understanding come from?
- What are we assuming about the community, the problem, or the proposed solution?
- What could our partner help us understand that we cannot learn from documentation alone?
- Which project decisions require their input?

Pay particular attention to knowledge gained through experience: how people navigate services, why earlier initiatives succeeded or struggled, how sensitive information is interpreted, and what makes an approach appropriate in this setting.

For example, a team developing a racism detection tool could ask its partner how context affects the interpretation of newspaper language. A team developing a resource platform could ask how people currently find support and how staff determine whether service information is still accurate. These conversations can reveal requirements that a generic feature list would miss.

### Prepare critically if you use AI

Generative AI can help brainstorm questions or identify gaps in a draft interview plan. Review its suggestions against the project materials and your partner's context. Treat AI-generated descriptions of community needs as assumptions to investigate. Your team remains responsible for deciding what to ask and interpreting what you hear.

## B. Planning and Scheduling the Interview

### Agree on the right participants

Ask your community partner who can best help answer your questions. Depending on the topic, this may include a project lead, frontline staff member, volunteer, domain expert, or someone with lived experience relevant to the project.

Choose participants according to the knowledge needed. A manager may explain organizational priorities, while someone doing the work may explain everyday practices and exceptions. Your main contact can help identify other perspectives and appropriate ways to involve them. One person's account should not be treated as representing an entire community.

### Agree on the right team members

You are working in large teams, not all of you should attend an interview, as that is overwhelming for your interviewees. Decide carefully which one(s) of you will conduct the interview. Typically 2 is enough, one to facilitate the interview and another to record notes or observations. Both of you must be comfortable facilitating an interview with excellent spoken communication. 

### Prepare a focused interview plan

Arrange your questions in a logical sequence: begin with the partner's work and community context, then explore current practices, needs, priorities, and possible project implications. Select the questions most relevant to the current stage of your project rather than trying to cover everything in one meeting.

Share a short agenda in advance that explains:

- The purpose of the interview and the topics you hope to understand.
- Who will attend and each person's role.
- The expected duration and proposed meeting format.
- Any materials or examples you would find helpful, if readily available.

Ask about scheduling, accessibility, and communication preferences. Assign a main interviewer and a note-taker so someone can focus fully on the conversation. If a graduate student or mentor joins, agree on their role beforehand.

Respect the partner's capacity. Coordinate questions across your team, review previous notes, and avoid sending several separate requests for the same information.

## C. Opening and Closing the Interview

### Opening the interview

Introduce your team and explain how the conversation will inform your INSPIRE project. Acknowledge the partner's expertise and invite them to correct your understanding.

For example:

> We are the INSPIRE student team working with you on [project]. We have reviewed [materials], and today we would like to understand [topic] from your experience. This will help us decide [project decision]. Please correct our assumptions or raise anything important that our questions miss.

Confirm the time available, explain how notes will be used and who will see them, and ask whether anything should remain outside the shared project record. Obtain permission before recording or using an AI transcription tool, explaining how it handles the conversation. Agree on an alternative if the partner prefers notes only.

Begin with something familiar, such as their role or a typical situation they encounter. Give them space to describe their work in their own terms.

### Closing the interview

Allow time to summarize the main points and explain your current interpretation. Invite corrections before discussing next steps.

Useful closing questions include:

- What have we misunderstood or missed?
- What should our team keep in mind as we make the next project decisions?
- Whose perspective would help us understand this more fully?
- What would make our next conversation useful to you?

Confirm any agreed actions, who will take them, and the preferred way to follow up. Thank your partner for their time and the knowledge they have shared. Be clear about which ideas your team will explore and which commitments have actually been agreed.

## D. Conducting the Interview

### Start with open-ended questions

Open-ended questions invite the partner to explain experiences, priorities, and reasoning in their own words. Ask one question at a time and follow up on important details. Use the following examples as a question bank, adapting them to your project and the person you are interviewing.

#### Understanding the community and the partner's work

- Tell us about your work and the people or communities involved.
- What would you want students new to this setting to understand before developing software for it?
- How do you learn about the needs and priorities of the people you work with?
- Where might people's experiences or needs differ within this community?

#### Learning from community partner knowledge

- Could you walk us through a recent example that illustrates the situation this project should address, without identifying anyone?
- What do you notice in these situations that someone unfamiliar with this work might overlook?
- What approaches have you tried before, and what did you learn from them?
- Are there existing practices, relationships, or resources that we should build on?
- What examples or materials could help us develop a better understanding?

#### Understanding current practices and needs

- How is this work done now, including any informal steps or workarounds?
- What works well and should be preserved?
- Where do difficulties arise, and what happens when they do?
- Which parts of the work require judgment or knowledge that would be difficult to capture in software?
- What would meaningful improvement look like for your organization and for the people affected?

#### Exploring values, risks, and priorities

- What needs to be protected or respected as we develop this project?
- Who might find the proposed approach difficult to access or use?
- What could go wrong, and who would be affected?
- If we can address only a few needs this semester, which matter most, and why?

#### Drawing on partner expertise to evaluate software and AI

- What would help you judge whether an output or recommendation is useful and appropriate?
- Could you share a difficult or ambiguous example that we should consider when testing?
- If AI contributes to the system, which outputs would need human review, and what knowledge would the reviewer need?
- Where could an AI-generated answer appear reasonable but miss something important about this context?

#### Planning continued collaboration

- At which points would your input be most useful as the project develops?
- What would make it easy for you to give feedback on our requirements or prototypes?
- What would need to be in place for someone to use and maintain the software after the semester?

### Follow the explanation, not just the question list

When a partner mentions something unexpected, explore it. Useful prompts include “Could you give us an example?”, “What happened next?”, and “What makes that distinction important?”

Ask about the need behind a proposed feature before discussing implementation. If a partner requests a dashboard, for example, ask what decisions it should support, who would use it, and what information those people currently rely on.

Use closed questions to clarify specific facts: “Is the information updated weekly?” or “Does every submission need staff review?” They are helpful for confirmation, but should be followed by an invitation to explain exceptions when relevant.

### Use clear, neutral language

Avoid questions that imply a preferred answer or assume the current approach is inadequate.

| Instead of asking | Ask |
| --- | --- |
| Wouldn't an app make this easier? | How do people currently do this, and where do they encounter difficulties? |
| Why is your process so inefficient? | Could you walk us through the process and explain what each step makes possible? |
| Would AI solve this problem? | Which parts of this work could benefit from software support, and which depend on human judgment? |
| Do you like our design? | How would this design work in a typical situation? Where would it fall short? |

Use your partner's terminology and ask when its meaning is unclear. Avoid software jargon that makes the conversation harder to participate in.

### Listen actively and check your interpretation

Let your partner do most of the talking. Pause after questions and allow time to think. Show attention in ways appropriate to the person and setting; do not assume that eye contact or body posture has the same meaning for everyone.

Restate important points in your own words and invite correction. For example:

> It sounds as though knowing whether information is current matters more than including every possible service. What have I missed in that understanding?

Keep the partner's meaning distinct from your proposed solution. If they say “People sometimes arrive when a service is unavailable,” do not immediately record “The system needs live availability tracking.” First explore why this happens, how availability is checked, and what responses would be feasible.

When perspectives differ, ask about the experiences and constraints behind each account. Acknowledge what you hear without taking sides or promising to resolve every concern.

### Take useful notes

Keep notes brief enough to support listening. Record examples, priorities, reasoning, uncertainties, and agreed actions. Clearly distinguish what the partner said from your team's interpretation and ideas.

Avoid recording unnecessary identifying details about community members. Before using interview material in an AI tool, confirm that the intended use is consistent with the permissions and data-handling arrangements for the project.

## E. Following Up and Applying What You Learned

### Return your understanding to the partner

Organize your notes promptly and share a concise summary through the agreed channel. Highlight the main needs and priorities, your interpretation of their implications, unresolved questions, and agreed next steps. Invite corrections and make follow-up questions specific and easy to answer.

A lack of response should remain an unconfirmed interpretation, rather than being recorded as agreement.

### Make the partner's knowledge visible in project decisions

Discuss as a team what changed because of the conversation. Update the relevant requirements, value considerations, sprint plan, GitHub issues, or decision records. Link decisions to the partner insight that informed them, using appropriate access controls for the notes.

For example, if a partner explains that staff cannot maintain daily information updates, reconsider a design that depends on daily manual entry. Record the constraint, explore feasible alternatives, and check your proposed response with the partner.

Ask yourselves:

- Which assumptions did the interview confirm, challenge, or leave unresolved?
- How did the partner's expertise change our requirements, priorities, design, or evaluation approach?
- Whose perspective is still missing?
- What will we bring back for feedback, and when?

Continue drawing on community partner knowledge across the sprints. Use concrete scenarios, prototypes, and test results to make feedback easier. When a suggestion cannot be implemented, explain the constraint and discuss alternatives. Show the partner how their contribution has influenced the work and give them opportunities to refine your understanding as the project develops.
