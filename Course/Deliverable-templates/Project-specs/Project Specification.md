# SENG480B Project Specification

## Project Context

This course is a **community-engaging, project-based experiential course**. You will work in a team on a real-world, socially meaningful project with authentic stakeholders, constraints, uncertainty, and changing needs.

The goal is not only to produce a strong technical outcome. You will also learn how to:

- work effectively in a cross-functional team;
- make decisions when information is incomplete;
- communicate with stakeholders;
- connect technical choices to user and organizational value;
- respond to feedback and changing priorities;
- evaluate trade-offs;
- use generative and agentic AI responsibly within a software development workflow; and
- take professional responsibility for the quality and impact of your team's work.

## Community-Engaged Software Development

This is not a typical class project. Each team will work on a real project developed in collaboration with a real **community partner** whose organization, users, and broader community have genuine needs, constraints, priorities, and values. Ideally, your community partners should be able to adopt what you have built this semester in their organizations, beyond this course - **your work can have a real impact!**

This means that prject success cannot be defined only by whether your team builds technically functional software. Your work should contribute meaningfully to the goals of the community partners and respond to the context in which the technology will actually be used.


Community partners are not simply sources of requirements. They bring **domain expertise, lived experience, organizational knowledge, and perspectives** that your technical team may not possess. Effective software engineering therefore requires your team to learn from that expertise and incorporate it into your decisions.

You should also consider the different groups affected by the technology you build. The goals of the partner organization, the needs of the people who will use the system, and the interests of the broader community may not always be identical. Part of your responsibility is to identify these perspectives and consider whose needs, values, and experiences are represented in project decisions.

Working with a real partner also involves uncertainty. Requirements may be incomplete or difficult to elicit easily, priorities may change, technical constraints may emerge, and the most useful outcome may differ from what your team originally imagined. Part of your responsibility is to navigate that uncertainty collaboratively and make justified decisions about how the project should evolve.

The goal is to create technology **with communities, rather than simply for them**, and to understand software engineering as a practice that can create meaningful social value.

## Software Development in the Age of AI

Generative and agentic AI are changing how software is designed, built, tested, documented, evaluated, and maintained.

Throughout this course, you will be expected to explore how AI can function as part of a software development team. Depending on the task, AI may act as an advisor, collaborator, reviewer, generator, or agent capable of completing multiple steps toward a goal.

Using AI effectively requires more than prompting a tool and accepting its output. Your team will need to decide:

- what work should be delegated to AI;
- what work should remain primarily human;
- how much autonomy AI should have;
- how AI-generated work will be reviewed and verified;
- what evidence is required before AI-generated work can be trusted;
- how AI affects team roles, knowledge, and decision-making; and
- how AI use may affect users, stakeholders, and the communities your project serves.

Your team remains responsible for the work you submit, including work produced with AI assistance.

> **AI use expectations:**  
> _Your team has autonomy over when and how to use AI. The sprint planning documents will help you to plan your use of AI, and the spring retrospective documents will help you reflect on whether or not this usage was effective._

## Project Structure of Iterative Development

Your project will be completed iteratively across a series of 3 sprints.

Each sprint should involve:

1. **Planning** what the team intends to accomplish with the help of your Sprint Planning documents
2. **Executing** the planned technical and project work;
3. **Reviewing and demonstrating** what was produced; and
4. **Reflecting** on what worked, what did not, how the team worked together, and how AI contributed in the form of a Sprint Retrospective.

Sprint planning and retrospectives should be treated seriously. They are opportunities to make decisions deliberately, assess whether those decisions worked, and adapt the team's approach over time.

> **Sprint structure:**  
> Your team must use the provided sprint planning and retrospective documents each sprint.

## Teamwork Is Part of the Technical Work

Software is rarely developed by individuals working independently. Real software projects require people with different expertise, experiences, priorities, and perspectives to make decisions together.

For this reason, **team dynamics are a central part of the course**

You are expected to contribute to a team environment where members:

- communicate openly;
- share information and technical knowledge;
- raise concerns when they disagree;
- ask questions when something is unclear;
- give and receive constructive feedback;
- distribute work responsibly;
- support one another in understanding the team's work;
- make decisions collectively when appropriate; and
- remain accountable to the team and its stakeholders.

A technically strong project produced through poor collaboration is not equivalent to effective software engineering practice.

## Effective Collaboration is Essential!

Collaboration is a core part of professional software development and an important part of this course.

Your work should not move directly from **created** to **accepted** simply because a team member, or an AI system, produced something that looks plausible.

Throughout the project, you will be expected to review one another's work. This may include reviewing:

- requirements;
- design decisions;
- architecture decisions;
- code;
- tests;
- AI-generated artifacts;
- documentation;
- analyses;
- stakeholder-facing materials; and
- strategic or technical recommendations.

Collaborating on tasks, and reviewing each other's work helps identify errors, surface assumptions, improve quality, and distribute knowledge across the team. It also helps prevent one person, or one AI system, from becoming the sole source of understanding for an important part of the project.

When reviewing AI-generated work, collaboration is especially important. AI output may be fluent and convincing while still being incomplete, incorrect, inappropriate for the project context, or inconsistent with stakeholder needs.

Therefore, your team should therefore focus not only on **whether something works**, but also on whether it is appropriate, justified, understandable, and aligned with the goals of the project.

> **Peer review requirements:**  
> The course deliverables include a graded peer review component so you can evaluate the collaboration skills of your fellow teammates.

## Connecting Technology to Value

A successful project is not defined only by how much software your team produces.

Your team should be able to explain:

- what problem you are solving;
- what values your stakeholders possess; 
- who benefits from the solution;
- what value the project creates;
- how requirements connect to stakeholder needs;
- why major technical decisions were made;
- how architecture and implementation choices support project goals;
- what trade-offs were considered; and
- how the technical solution aligns with the broader strategy and purpose of the project.

You should expect to revisit these questions throughout the course as your understanding of the problem develops.

> **Value and strategy expectations:**  
> _You will maintain a Tech-Value alignment document throughout the semester to capture how the technology you build connects to your project's values._

# Accountability for AI-Assisted Work

AI use does not transfer responsibility away from the student or team.

If AI contributes to an artifact, your team should be able to explain:

- what AI contributed;
- why its use was appropriate;
- how the output was evaluated;
- what changes humans made;
- what limitations or risks were identified; and
- why the final output was accepted.

You may be asked to explain or defend AI-assisted work during project reviews, demos, presentations, or other course activities.

> **Documentation expectations:**  
> _This understanding should be clear from your regular completion of sprint documentation, ADRs, and the tech-value alignment document._

# Project Success

By the end of the course, **success should mean more than having a working piece of software.**

You should leave the course with experience in:

- building software for a real-world context;
- working through ambiguity with a team;
- making and defending engineering decisions;
- connecting technical work to stakeholder and social value;
- reviewing the work of humans and AI;
- deciding when AI should and should not be trusted with a task;
- adapting your process based on evidence and reflection; and
- taking responsibility for software developed in an AI-enabled environment.

The objective is to develop the judgment required to build software **with AI, with other people, and for people**.
